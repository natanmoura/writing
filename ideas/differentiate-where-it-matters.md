# Working title: AI won't hollow out game dev evenly

## Thesis
> What matters to different products and what people will care about is not evenly distributed. It is jagged. Some products will not benefit from a better 3D model. Some products will live and die on whether they can deliver on those. In the same way that some products will live and die by the game feel of the kickback of a gun, and others will not.

**So what (from dump 6):** Find the layer, or the combination, that differentiates your product. Automating it is fine, but only if the result reaches exceptional quality. The danger is defaulting to AI for knowledge and output in that layer: AI gives you the consensus answer, and consensus can't differentiate. It's fine where good enough is the goal, but people misjudge what good enough means, and the market notices.

## Condensed argument (after 7 dumps)

**Thesis:** AI won't hollow out product-making evenly, because what buyers care about is jagged and product-specific. In whatever layer differentiates a product, the real risk isn't using AI. It's AI's pull toward the consensus answer.

1. Products win by differentiating on something enough buyers care about.
2. That can be any layer, from lore and vibe to low-level feel, and which one varies by product (*Undertale* vs. *Overwatch*).
3. Buyers feel quality in aggregate even when they can't articulate it, so the qualitative layers matter more than metrics show (iPhone scrolling, app churn).
4. Layers that only need to be adequate can be automated. But adequate isn't generic, and people routinely misjudge what good enough means.
5. AI is fine in the differentiating layer only if the result is exceptional. By default, AI produces the consensus answer, and consensus doesn't differentiate.
6. Sometimes the differentiator is restraint (South Park). Taste is a perspective, "I like this, not that," and models trained on aggregate preference don't have one unless a person steers them.
7. So the hollowing-out falls on the commercial middle tier, and on teams that can't tell which layer matters.

## Flaws, ranked by damage

1. **The thesis is circular as written.** [Certain] Winners won on quality people can't articulate, and losers lacked quality they couldn't see, so any outcome confirms it. *Fix:* include one case where the feel-based explanation predicts something the metrics don't, such as a feature-complete game that flopped on feel, or a game that won despite weak features.
2. **The argument drifts from jobs to advice.** [Likely] *(new)* Dump 1 asks which human work AI replaces. The conclusion is product-strategy advice for creators. The labor question goes unanswered. *Fix:* add a section on craft roles. Specialists survive where their layer is the differentiator, but studios often can't tell which layer that is in advance, which makes those jobs precarious rather than safe.
3. **Every example is a hit.** [Certain] *(new)* Undertale, Elden Ring, Overwatch, iPhone, Celeste. You never show polished games that failed, which suggests survivorship bias. *Fix:* add one or two well-crafted flops and explain why the thesis still holds.
4. **"Make what matters excellent" collapses into "be good."** [Certain] *Fix:* lead with what's distinctive: jaggedness and the consensus pull.
5. **No forward-looking test.** [Likely] "Find your layer" only works in hindsight and doesn't cover products that win on the sum of their parts. *Fix:* offer "what the player does most" as a starting heuristic, and add sum-of-parts as a second branch.
6. **Distribution and economics are missing.** [Likely] *(new)* Cheap AI-made adequacy floods the market, so discovery and marketing may become the bottleneck more than quality. And lower costs can make thinner differentiation profitable. *Fix:* acknowledge this and say why quality still decides the outcome once players find the game.
7. **"LLMs codify language" is outdated.** [Certain] Models are multimodal, and dump 7 concedes AI can derive game feel from video. *Fix:* use the aggregate-preference argument instead.
8. **Track record clashes with misattribution, and the prompter claim is gatekeeping.** [Likely] If success is misattributed, a few hits don't prove discernment, and discernment lives in the person, not the tool. *Fix:* "AI floods the market with output that has no discernment behind it, so discernment becomes the scarce, trusted signal."
9. **"All cylinders" clashes with restraint.** [Certain, easy fix] *Fix:* all cylinders means every layer is right, not maximal.
10. **Scope says products, evidence says games.** [Certain, minor] *Fix:* frame it as a games article with app and iPhone asides, or add non-game evidence.

## Angle
The popular prediction is that AI automates low-level craft and leaves high-level vision to humans. That's too prescriptive. Products win by differentiating on what their buyers care about, and that can be any layer, so AI's impact will be jagged and specific to each product.

## Core argument
1. Products succeed by differentiating on a dimension enough people care about.
2. Any layer can be that dimension: lore and vibe (*Undertale*, *Lethal Company*) or low-level feel (*Overwatch*, *Celeste*, *Cuphead*).
3. Layers that only need to be adequate are where AI saves the most, but "adequate" often means specific and deliberate, not generic.
3a. Automating the differentiating layer is fine if it reaches exceptional quality. The trap is that AI defaults to consensus, and consensus doesn't differentiate.
4. Quality is felt in aggregate. Many small, adequate-but-careless choices add up to something that doesn't smell like quality (iPhone, app churn).
5. Differentiation can also be restraint: a limitation pushed to its conclusion (South Park). Taste is choosing what not to do, and more production value isn't automatically better.
6. So AI hits the commercial middle tier hardest. Masterpieces stay human-intensive because they fire on every layer.

A starting point for writing. These are the ideas that are Natan's own or fresh twists on familiar ones. ## Article A: AI and deciding what matters

*Most developed. 8 parts of material, a condensed argument and a ranked flaws list in ideas/jagged-differentiation.md.*

### A1. AI pulls you toward consensus in exactly the layer where you need to diverge [Fresh]
- **Claim:** Using AI in the layer that differentiates your product is fine if the result is exceptional. The real danger is that AI-sourced knowledge and output default to the consensus answer, and consensus can't differentiate.
- **Why it's non-obvious:** The AI debate is usually framed as "which jobs does AI replace?" This reframes it as "what does AI do to the *distinctiveness* of what you make?" The risk isn't low quality, it's averageness.
- **Best example:** Asking an AI what makes game feel good and implementing the answer gets you the industry-standard version, which is fine for an adequate layer and fatal for the differentiating one.
- **Industry-level version (part 9):** when everyone uses the same tools, thousands of small defaults converge across the whole market. That's "convergence by micro-decision."
- **Needs:** One concrete case of a product that went generic this way. Also the mechanism stated precisely: models are trained toward aggregate preference, and taste is one perspective's "I like this, not that."
- **Source:** topics/jagged-differentiation.md (parts 6–7)

### A2. Adequate isn't generic [Fresh]
- **Claim:** The layers a product "only needs to be adequate at" are often still specific and deliberate. Swapping in generic output breaks them.
- **Why it's non-obvious:** The standard automation argument assumes non-differentiating layers are interchangeable. They often aren't: *Lethal Company*'s crude visuals are part of its horror-comedy vibe.
- **Best example:** *Lethal Company*, *Undertale*'s sprites.
- **Needs:** A test for telling a deliberately-adequate layer from a truly interchangeable one.
- **Source:** topics/jagged-differentiation.md (part 2 plus Claude's notes)

### A3. Each detail is measurable, but the aggregate isn't [Twist]
- **Claim:** Lag, frame drops and button response are all measurable. What isn't measurable is the combined impression ("it smells like someone gave a damn"), which is what people actually respond to. Teams optimize the parts they can see on a dashboard.
- **Why it's non-obvious:** It resolves the usual "qualitative vs. quantitative" argument. Both sides are partly right, and the failure is at the level of the sum.
- **Best example:** iPhone rubber-band scrolling and responsiveness; apps people quit for "feeling off."
- **Needs:** A case where the aggregate predicted something the individual metrics didn't. This also fixes the article's biggest flaw (the circularity).
- **Source:** topics/jagged-differentiation.md (part 4)

### A4. Craft jobs are precarious, not safe or doomed [Fresh]
- **Claim:** Because the layer that matters is different for every product, craft specialists survive where their layer is the differentiator. But studios often can't tell which layer that is before shipping, so these roles become precarious rather than clearly safe or clearly replaced.
- **Why it's non-obvious:** Most takes say either "craft is dead" or "craft is safe." This says the risk depends on each product and is hard to predict.
- **Best example:** A gun-feel animator is essential on *Overwatch* and dispensable on a narrative game.
- **Sharpened by part 9:** top studios keep craft to protect franchises, and the middle hollows out. So craft work concentrates at the top, with fewer, more competitive seats.
- **Needs:** Natan's own experience of how studios decide which roles to cut. This answers the question the dump started with.
- **Source:** Claude's synthesis of topics/jagged-differentiation.md (flaw 2)

### A5. Cheap production raises the value of verifiable human provenance [Twist]
- **Claim:** As AI makes competent output cheap, human perspective and story become the differentiating layer, the luxury mechanism applied to ordinary products. Since AI also makes fake stories cheap, what's scarce is *verifiable* provenance and track record.
- **Why it's non-obvious:** "Human-made will matter more" is common. The verifiability and longevity part is the twist.
- **Best example:** None yet.
- **Needs:** An example, and an answer to how a new creator builds verifiable provenance.
- **Source:** topics/high-ticket-and-signaling.md (Claude's notes)

## Article B: Show up where other things aren't

*Four parts of material in topics/fitting-into-someones-life.md.*

### B0. Reframe the category, and you change the budget it competes with [Fresh]
- **Claim:** A Friend Slop game pitched as "a night in instead of a night out" competes with a restaurant dinner, not with other games. The same product looks cheap next to the new anchor.
- **Why it's non-obvious:** The usual advice is to pick a better category. This says you can move an existing product into a different category through framing alone, and the price anchor moves with it.
- **Best example:** Friend Slop games vs. a restaurant dinner with friends.
- **Needs:** Evidence that players actually think about it this way, and an explanation of why the night-in slot is less contested than the game slot.
- **Source:** topics/fitting-into-someones-life.md (part 5)

### B1. A slot is purpose, place and social context, not just category [Fresh]
- **Claim:** Products don't just compete within categories or for time. They compete for a specific combination of *why* (leisure vs. goal), *where* (couch vs. on the go) and *with whom* (alone vs. shown off). Change any one and you may be out of the crowded slot.
- **Why it's non-obvious:** Time-slot thinking usually treats all leisure time as one pool. A Tamagotchi is still leisure, but it wins because of place and social context.
- **Best example:** A Tamagotchi on a bag vs. a game in the evening. Second example: Friend Slop reframed as a night in (B0).
- **Needs:** One example outside games and toys.
- **Source:** topics/fitting-into-someones-life.md (parts 1–2)

### B2. Compete for moments, not hours: time spent is the wrong metric [Fresh]
- **Claim:** People pay for value per moment, not for hours captured. A product can own a ten-second moment (waking up) or a once-a-year one (wearing a watch) and be worth a lot.
- **Why it's non-obvious:** Much of the industry optimizes for engagement time. This argues that metric hides where value is.
- **Best example:** Premium alarm clocks (Hatch Restore, Loftie), a luxury watch worn once a year.
- **Needs:** Natan's endorsement of the framing. It's implied in part 3, not stated.
- **Source:** topics/fitting-into-someones-life.md (part 3)

### B3. Hardware's edge is its category, not its moat [Twist]
- **Claim:** People move into hardware for the friction-as-moat. The real advantage is that hardware is often a complement to screen time, not a substitute, so it isn't fighting Netflix.
- **Why it's non-obvious:** It overturns the usual reason given for hardware.
- **Best example:** A Tamagotchi bought on top of games, not instead of them.
- **Needs:** It still competes for wallet and the phone's role, and kids now carry phones. Address both.
- **Source:** topics/fitting-into-someones-life.md (part 2)

### B4. The form you show up in is part of the differentiation [Twist]
- **Claim:** The same job done as an object vs. an app puts you in a completely different competitive field.
- **Best example:** An alarm clock as a physical object vs. an alarm app.
- **Needs:** The physical market isn't empty, only the quality tier is. State it that way.
- **Source:** topics/fitting-into-someones-life.md (part 3)

### B5. Goal time comes from a separate budget [Twist, weakest]
- **Claim:** Things people do because a goal matters to them (learning sign language) don't take from the leisure budget.
- **Needs:** Motivation decay. Goal apps lose most users within weeks. Without an answer, this one may not hold up.
- **Source:** topics/fitting-into-someones-life.md (part 1)

## Article C (or a section of A): Making the act of creating the product

### C1. When AI lowers the barrier to making, the fun of making becomes the product [Twist]
- **Claim:** Products where creating is the enjoyable part (Minecraft) turn users into authors and evangelists. As AI does more of the making, products that keep the *act* enjoyable gain value.
- **Best example:** Minecraft. Counterexample: Dreams.
- **Needs:** Why Minecraft worked and Dreams didn't. And how the business serves the roughly 90% who don't create.
- **Source:** topics/product-as-creative-tool.md

## Article D: AI and human art: what AI can't keep

*Seven parts of material, from topics/ai-and-human-art.md. Outline in ideas/ai-and-human-art.md.*

### D1. AI erodes the exact knowledge it can't encode [Fresh]
- **Claim:** AI keeps the documented layer of a craft. The tacit layer (feel, edge cases, in-the-moment specificity) is passed on only by apprenticeship, and AI removes the market reason to apprentice. So the knowledge most at risk is the knowledge that makes work distinctive.
- **Why it's non-obvious:** "AI can't do feel" and "AI pulls up the ladder" are each common. Putting them together shows the loss isn't random: it falls on the differentiating layer.
- **Best example:** Natan's drawing tool (feel AI can't derive) + "what is animation actually like if everyone is just auto-generating animation?"
- **Needs:** A real field where this already happened.
- **Source:** topics/ai-and-human-art.md (parts 4, 5, 7, Claude's synthesis). Links to A1 and A4.

### D2. Knowledge-loss cycles run at the speed of technology [Twist]
- **Claim:** History loses and rediscovers knowledge. What's new is that the cycle speeds up with tech, and rediscovery depends on keepers (the Renaissance relied on texts preserved elsewhere).
- **Needs:** The Renaissance fact correction, and a modern example.
- **Source:** topics/ai-and-human-art.md (part 7)

### D3. Perspective lives in a body of work, and AI produces pieces [Twist]
- **Claim:** You understand an artist across all their paintings, not one. A single AI output can compete with a single human piece. A coherent perspective across many works is where human time compounds.
- **Needs:** A test: can curating many AI outputs build a body of work?
- **Source:** topics/ai-and-human-art.md (part 2). Pairs with the track-record point under A5.

### D4. Authorship is the decisions you made [Twist]
- **Claim:** One-shotting is "Russian roulette": fun, but nothing in it you can communicate or put a spin on. Expression requires staying in the loop.
- **Source:** topics/ai-and-human-art.md (parts 4–5)

*Left out as familiar:* cheap results re-value the handmade; "it's a capitalism problem"; don't gatekeep taste; the genius/daimon framing (Gilbert's TED talk). The genius framing can open the piece but shouldn't carry it.

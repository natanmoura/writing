# High-ticket items, signaling and collections

**Source:** raw/2026-11-24-high-ticket-and-signaling.md
**Related:** topics/jagged-differentiation.md, topics/fitting-into-someones-life.md

## Core idea
Once a product is priced far above its category, it becomes a different product. It's no longer selling utility. It's selling story, craft, exclusivity and the signal of owning it. Value is set by perception: tastemakers buy first and evangelize, third-party validation drives price, and longevity turns perception into trust. In categories that aren't justified by utility, the only ceiling on price is how good the story is and whether the way of doing things is differentiated in a way people care about.

## Key points

### A different product, not a pricier one
- Differentiating "on a vector that basically no one else is playing" makes it "effectively a different product."
- Most sunglasses sell under about $200. A $5,000 pair creates its own market. "They're not winning on sunglasses. They're winning on a story, a narrative, on craft, and exclusivity."
- "The high-ticket item defies category, because often the high-ticket item is more about signaling than it is about utility." Expensive watches mostly "sit in a cabinet."

### Collections and perception
- Past utility, with enough price and exclusivity, "we are in the realm of collections," which "often defies logic."
- Value "comes down to who is willing to pay a certain amount for something."

### Tastemakers and third-party validation
- Tastemakers buy first, "and then everyone else decides that they were right."
- The seller usually isn't the tastemaker. The seller understands and tells the story. The tastemaker is "the first person who cares about that story enough to evangelize it."
- "We trust people more who evangelize something that they didn't make." A third party saying it's great and paying for it "is essentially the validation and signal that can drive the price."

### Hype vs. longevity
- Perception enables "weird games": hype and "exclusivity that is frankly short-lived."
- Items with real staying power have "stood the test of time." Longevity builds trust that something is "intrinsically, qualitatively differentiated... not just a one-off fluke."
- A high-ticket item is "the combination of this perception, but also the trust and the longevity."

### Art markets
- Art works the same way. Its value is very specific, and not everyone shares it, but among those who do there are "several other buyers who will value it at that price."
- Art is a snapshot "of a time, of an era, of a way of seeing the world." Owning it signals how much you care about those things.

### Why buyers pay
- Wealthy buyers often value what they can't make themselves. Natan describes "the rich living vicariously through the more authentic lives of people who are ordinary," calling it "a type of a fetishization."
- "Sometimes it is pure signal": showing the group you also have taste.

### No ceiling outside utility
- In categories not justified by utility, "there's effectively no limit to how high you can sell. The only limit is how good of a story you can... make. And whether you have a differentiated way of doing things that people care about."

## Tensions and contradictions
- **Perception vs. "intrinsic" quality.** The dump says value is perception, then says longevity proves something is "intrinsically, qualitatively differentiated." Longevity could also be perception that's been kept up for a long time. Which is it?
- **Story vs. differentiation.** The last line says the limit is the story *and* "a differentiated way of doing things." Is the story enough, or does it need real craft underneath?

## Open questions
- What makes some high-ticket items last and others fade? That's the real question in this dump, and it isn't answered yet.
- Can a new product build the trust that longevity gives, or is that only available to old brands?
- How does this apply to digital products? Is there a high-ticket game or app?

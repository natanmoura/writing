# Fitting into someone's life: competing for time

**Source:** raw/2026-11-24-fitting-into-someones-life.md
**Related:** topics/jagged-differentiation.md, topics/high-ticket-and-signaling.md, topics/product-as-creative-tool.md

## Core idea
**Thesis, in Natan's words:** "You want to show up in a place where other things are not, and then deliver something that people actually care about."

Every product, digital or not, has to fit into someone's life, so it's competing for a slice of their time. Entertainment (games, movies, YouTube, doomscrolling) all fights over the same leisure slot, where winners take most and people converge socially on the same few things. Products that solve a specific pain point for a specific group can step outside that slot. The audience is smaller, but the product draws on time people set aside for something that matters to them.

## Key points

### The leisure slot
- The real question is "how do we build products in this age?" Products are "something fundamentally that fits into someone's life."
- If your category is already crowded for that time, "it is actually an uphill battle."
- Games compete with movies, YouTube and doomscrolling, all "on a very similar slot." The everyday choice is "am I going to play a game. Am I going to watch a movie, or am I going to go watch something on YouTube?"
- Doomscrolling is "effectively a slot machine," which makes it hard to pull people away.

### Winner-take-most through social convergence
- People have limited hours, so they play "not only the best quality thing, they're going to play the thing that other people are playing."
- "We are fundamentally social creatures." Converging on the same games and releases is "a tribal thing" and "a type of a social validation."

### Niching out of the slot
- Some categories "step out of areas that are under high competition," and Natan encourages more ideas there.
- Solving "a specific pain point for a specific group" means "niching down into an area where other people simply are not."
- **Example:** a sign-language learning app. The demographic is smaller, but for some people it's important enough that they'll do it "outside of the window where they would be watching Netflix, YouTube, or playing a game."

### Hardware: the advantage is the category, not the moat
- People move into hardware believing the friction is a moat. Natan: "the benefit of competing on hardware is not specific to the moat. It's actually specific to the category."
- Hardware like an alarm clock or a Tamagotchi isn't bought *instead of* games. It's bought "on top of" them. "It is not competing on other categories."
- A keychain toy on your bag is "a different value proposition than a game they're going to sit down and play in the evening" against YouTube and Netflix.
- **Why it competes differently:** a younger audience with more off-screen time, sharing it in the schoolyard; it's "with you on the go"; you can "show off" with it out in the world.
- The unit economics may be bad, but the opportunity comes from "the category and how it fits into your day."
- Phones are what we use out in the world, but some objects are "simply just cool... fundamentally something different than a phone," delivering an experience "in a place where other things are not."

### Value isn't tied to time spent
- There's "a lot of value to be had in identifying things that people will pay for, need to use, but don't necessarily spend an inordinate amount of time using."
- A watch worn once a year can sell for hundreds of thousands. The value is in collecting, the conversations, and "the trading and community" that come with owning it.
- Many items fit "into other small moments in your day."

### The form you show up in is part of the differentiation
- Everyone needs an alarm clock. A phone can do it, but a physical clock that is "qualitatively unique and different" faces few competitors as an object. As an app, "you're in a much more crowded space."
- "The way in which you show up in a category actually matters and is part of the differentiation."

### Underexplored categories
- Many categories have no "delightful or unique or good experiences," and "the market has not even signaled that they want something high quality in that area."
- "That is the work of entrepreneurs": finding underexplored categories "that people actually value quite a lot" and whether something can be delivered there.

### Getting noticed vs. staying
- "Marketing is differentiation." Showing up unlike anyone else gets you noticed, and that newness is where the entrepreneurial risk is.
- "Getting noticed is one thing. Staying around is a different thing." Gimmicks "show up uniquely in a category. They win, and they're forgotten."
- "Even in a gimmick, there is often a seed of something yet to come."
- The Tamagotchi played on "negative human psychologies," the fear the pet will die, which may have driven the fad. But "a small digital friend that you can take with you" holds "seeds for many other products."
- **Recombination:** when new technology arrives, combining it with something else creates something new. Ideas: digital pets as a kind of social network, a game-like layer that creates network effects, more user-generated content. (The user-generated content thread continues in topics/product-as-creative-tool.md.)

### Reframing the category: Friend Slop vs. a restaurant (part 5)
- A one-evening game is "almost like going to a restaurant." Friends can go out to dinner, or "all pay the same amount of money, buy a game, chill, and have a little experience together."
- Recontextualized, a Friend Slop game shows up "almost in a different category." It replaces "hang out with people at a restaurant," not "when I would normally play games."
- "That's like a perception reframe of a category that... can make the value proposition sound better."

### Price expectations shift
- "Years ago, no one was paying like $10 for an app or monthly subscriptions," and now many apps do. "Maybe it was the ChatGPT subscription that kind of moved the needle."
- "The Overton window has shifted in terms of pricing." Prices can move depending on "what categories we start viewing as valuable, or in what time slot in our life we start using certain things for."

## Examples and anecdotes
- Sign-language learning app (niche, goal-driven).
- Tamagotchi-style keychain toy, alarm clock (hardware as an addition, not a substitute).
- Friend Slop games framed as a night in instead of a night out. ("Friend Slot" in the raw dump is a dictation slip for Friend Slop.)
- Luxury watch worn once a year (value from collecting and community, not use time).
- Premium alarm clocks: Hatch Restore, Loftie (suggested in chat; real examples supporting the alarm-clock point).
- Netflix / YouTube / games / doomscrolling as competitors for the same slot.
- Needed: a real niche product that won this way, and a real game that broke through the leisure slot without social convergence (or failed for lack of it).

## Tensions and contradictions
- **Time vs. moments.** Part 1 says products compete for time. Part 3 says value isn't tied to time spent. Possible resolution: you compete for a *moment* in someone's day, not for *hours*. A product can own a ten-second moment (waking up) and be worth a lot.
- None yet within this dump.
- **With the jagged-differentiation piece:** that piece says win by differentiating on what buyers care about. This one says pick a slot where you're not fighting everyone. They may be the same idea at two levels: *where* you compete vs. *how* you stand out once you're there.

## Open questions
- Which recent hardware toys or gadgets have succeeded this way, and which flopped?
- What exactly defines a "slot": purpose (leisure vs. goal), place (couch vs. on the go), or social context (alone vs. shown off)?
- Is goal-driven time really a separate budget, or just a different motivation drawing on the same hours?
- Can a niche product stay a niche, or does success attract the big general players?
- How does social convergence work in niche products? Is there a "tribe" for sign-language learners too?
- Where do non-digital products fit? The dump says "it's not just digital," but every example is digital.

## Claude's notes
- *[Claude]* **The time-slot framing is well known** (also raised in chat). Reed Hastings said Netflix competes with sleep [Certain], and "what job is this product hired for" is the jobs-to-be-done framework. The fresh angle is narrower: **goal-driven time comes from a different budget than leisure time**, so a product there doesn't compete with Netflix at all.
- *[Claude]* **Niches aren't uncontested** (also raised in chat). Sign-language learners already have general language apps aimed at them. "Where other people simply are not" overstates it. Better: where the *big* players aren't optimized for you.
- *[Claude]* **The goal budget has its own problem: motivation decays.** Language-learning and fitness apps are known for steep drop-off after the first weeks [Likely]. A product in the goal budget escapes the Netflix fight but swaps it for a retention fight against the user's own willpower. The article should face that, or the niche advice reads as too easy.
- *[Claude]* **Social convergence cuts both ways.** It explains why winners take most, but it also means a crowded slot isn't only uphill. It's high-variance: a game that catches the social wave wins far more than any niche can. So the advice depends on risk appetite, not just on crowding.
- *[Claude]* **Part 2 refines the model, and the article should say so.** Part 1 split time into leisure vs. goal-driven budgets. But a Tamagotchi is still leisure. It wins because of *where and with whom* it's used (on the go, in the schoolyard, shown to friends), not because of purpose. So a "slot" is defined by purpose, place *and* social context. That's a richer framing than "niche down."
- *[Claude]* **Economics names it: complements vs. substitutes** (also raised in chat). Hardware like this is a complement to evening screen time, not a substitute for it.
- *[Claude]* **It still competes, just elsewhere** (also raised in chat). It fights for wallet and gift budget, and for the phone's role while you're out. Kids increasingly carry phones, which weakens the schoolyard argument [Likely].
- *[Claude]* **The Tamagotchi example carries the hype problem from the high-ticket topic.** The original was a huge craze that faded fast [Likely]. Escaping the crowded slot gets you in the door, but it doesn't solve staying power. Worth connecting to "hype vs. longevity."
- *[Claude]* **Physical alarm clocks aren't uncrowded** (also raised in chat). Cheap ones are everywhere. What's thin is the *quality* tier, which Hatch Restore and Loftie occupy. The claim should be "few quality competitors," not "few competitors."
- *[Claude]* **"The market hasn't signaled it wants quality" cuts both ways.** Sometimes that's latent demand waiting for an entrepreneur. Often it means people genuinely won't pay more there. The article needs a way to tell the two apart, or it's survivorship bias again: we only remember the Hatches that found demand.
- *[Claude]* **There's a sharp, separate angle here: time spent is the wrong metric.** Much of the industry optimizes for engagement hours. Part 3 implies that value per moment, not hours captured, is what people pay for. That's a counter-position worth stating directly.
- *[Claude]* **"Marketing is differentiation" conflates being different with being noticed** (also raised in chat). Showing up differently gets attention, but that's distribution. Whether the difference is one people care about is the jagged-differentiation question. Keep the two apart.
- *[Claude]* **"Every gimmick holds a seed" is hindsight-friendly.** After the fact, you can find a seed in anything. The useful version: which part of the gimmick met a real, lasting motivation (portable companionship) and which part was novelty or manipulation (the fear of it dying)? That's a test you can apply before building.
- *[Claude]* **The restaurant reframe is the freshest idea in this topic** (also raised in chat). It also gives B1 its missing second example: the same game moves slots by changing the *social context and the budget* it draws from. The mechanism is price anchoring. Next to a $40 dinner, a $10 game looks cheap; next to other games, it looks like shovelware.
- *[Claude]* **The new slot is crowded too.** A night in with friends also competes with streaming together, board games and just going out. The reframe gets you out of the game slot, not out of competition. Say which slot you're moving into and why it's less contested.
- *[Claude]* **"ChatGPT moved the needle" is a guess** (also raised in chat). Subscription apps were common well before it [Certain]. Drop it, or turn it into a question.

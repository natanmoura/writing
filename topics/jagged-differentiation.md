# Jagged differentiation: deciding what matters in a product

**Source:** raw/2026-11-24-jagged-differentiation.md
**Related:** topics/fitting-into-someones-life.md, topics/high-ticket-and-signaling.md, topics/product-as-creative-tool.md, topics/ai-and-human-art.md (one dump, nine parts; "part N" below refers to those sections)

## Core idea
A product succeeds by being different along some dimension that enough buyers care about, and which dimension that is varies by product. It can be any layer, from lore and vibe down to the kickback of a gun. So AI's effect on product-making is jagged: it hollows out what a given product only needs to be adequate at. In the layer that differentiates the product, using AI is fine only if the result is exceptional, and the real risk is AI's pull toward the consensus answer. Deciding what matters, and having the taste and the team to execute it, is the skill that matters most.

Team composition and restraint/taste are part of this one idea, as subsections below: they're two ways of answering "how do you differentiate where it matters?"

## Key points

### Differentiation is jagged
- **Thesis, in Natan's words:** "What matters to different products or ideas and what people will care about is not some kind of evenly distributed thing. It is actually jagged. Some products will not benefit from a better 3D model. Some products will live and die on whether they can deliver on those. In the same way that some products will live and die by the game feel of the kickback of a gun, and others will not."
- **Scope is commercial, not artistic.** This is about making things to sell at a profit, where the incentive to cut corners lives.
- **The mechanism is differentiation.** "When a product is made, the reason it does well is because it is differentiating in some way on some vector that people actually care about."
- **Two ways to fail:** not being differentiated at all, or being differentiated on something people don't care about (or not enough people to be profitable). This is common in indie games.
- **The view being argued against:** that only high-level direction and concepting will matter, while low-level craft (3D modeling, animation, game feel) gets automated away. Natan calls this "too prescriptive across the board."
- **Counter-claim:** "everything in the game design stack can be a differentiator."
- **Practical consequence:** creators will "have to get smart about identifying what it is about what they're making that is differentiated."
- **Low-level differentiation is real.** "Certain games live and die on the low level." In many games, "the thing the player spends the most time doing is a low-level repetitive interaction." If that doesn't feel great, players like the game less, and "the games that do get those things right end up winning."
- **Juice, listed:** sword attack animations, game feel, gun kickback, gun animations, how characters accelerate when they move, effects animation.
- **Summary line:** "you need to differentiate in the place where it matters. And sometimes that differentiation is high level, and sometimes that differentiation is low level."
- **Masterpieces fire on all cylinders.** "The games that people love the most are often hitting on multiple cylinders, not just one." Work people call art or a masterpiece has high quality "from the top level all the way down to the low level."
- **Quality is felt in aggregate.** Small qualitative details don't matter individually, "but when you add them all up together, it smells like quality. It smells like I gave a fuck. And people have a very good nose for that."
- **Players sense what they can't articulate.** "They know Elden Ring looks sick, they don't know why." People are pattern-sensitive. Artists see the differences more clearly, "but if you think that the average player can't feel or sense those differences, you are severely wrong."
- **We measure the wrong things.** Teams talk about what's measurable (onboarding, value proposition, price), but players often stay for qualitative things.
- **We'll misattribute success.** "Things are going to win, and we're not going to know exactly why they won. We will attribute the wrong reasons."
- **Automation in the differentiating layer is fine if the bar is met.** "You don't need to protect it from automation, but if you are going to use automation for it, you need to make sure that it is at the actual quality that is extremely high." (This replaces the earlier "protect your differentiator from AI" framing.)
- **Diminishing returns:** past a certain point, more quality in a layer stops paying off.
- **Emergent differentiation:** some products win on "an entire ecosystem of things that aren't necessarily all that differentiated or polished, but the sum of all their parts" is differentiated as a whole.
- **Blind spots cause misattributed failure.** People cut corners, the product fails, and they blame something else "because they will not understand what they are missing qualitatively... It is a blind spot."
- **AI gives you the consensus answer.** People "get an AI to search up what they don't know, and they'll implement that. But if you are doing that in an area where you're trying to differentiate, you are not actually going to end up differentiating a whole lot." That only works where good enough is the goal.
- **"Good enough" is often not.** People who think something is good enough often ship work that is far from it, "not in a way that only experts understand, but in a way that the market also immediately clocks."
- **Why misattribution happens:** we can see where players put their money, but we can't measure the qualitative reasons, so we credit the wrong ones. Players rarely say "the gun shooting feels great." They say "it feels good," "it feels smooth." Only specialists can be more specific.
- **This holds at every layer, not just feel.** "Why is Elden Ring's design so cool and others are not so cool? People probably can't articulate." They know it's differentiated because "it feels like they're in a place that is specific."
- **Specificity comes from perspective:** "a perspective, or a collective group of people that are really honing in on a perspective."
- **The LLM problem, as Natan puts it:** LLMs "codify language," and much of what we like in games can't be articulated. AI can now sometimes derive the logic behind game feel from video, but asked why a design is good, "it doesn't have a perspective."
- **Adequacy layers:** in some games the low-level work "mostly needs to be adequate. Can't be broken, but it doesn't need to be revolutionary." Those layers are where automation saves time.

### Winner-take-all: why top studios keep craft (part 9)
- Returns follow a Pareto distribution. Top games earn so much that paying millions for handmade craft is worth it to protect "a reputation or a franchise." Cutting corners risks losing far more.
- AI tools can help, but top studios must be "very discerning about not losing the quality."
- **Convergence by micro-decision:** "if other people are using AI tools, then by them dipping into the AI tools as well, they are actually becoming more like the crowd." "All these micro decisions, they start converging on everything else that's out there."
- Natan's own caveat: "maybe that's only true for franchises."
- Demand is limited ("only a certain amount of games that make money in a year"), so with more games, the winners "are still going to be the ones that are the highest quality."
- The open bet: "does AI produce good enough quality that no one would notice?"
- **The middle hollows out**: indies and corner-cutters, though some will use AI "intelligently" to scale, and there will be "some massive hits as a result."
- **Retention is the judge:** players may try a game, but "do they come back day 2, 3, 4, 5, 6?" Without retention, it's "a flash in the pan."

### Restraint and taste
- "It's not just about going more quality on everything. Sometimes the differentiation is actually the limitation."
- Mastery and taste are "just as much about what you choose to do as what you choose not to do."
- "Often quality and differentiation is a byproduct of restraint. That restraint is something that a lot of people identify as taste."
- "The ability to explore a limitation, to its logical conclusion, is a powerful differentiator."
- "Just because you can add more production value doesn't mean you end up with a better product."
- "We can feel when there is discernment in a product." (Echoes the "smells like quality" point above.)
- **Taste is preference, not problem-solving.** "Perspectives, like taste, is not about just logically solving for something. It's about saying, I like this and I don't like that."
- **Success is agreement with that preference:** "whether something succeeds or not depends on whether other people agree that they like that more than something else."
- **Specificity comes from perspective.** A world feels like "a place that is specific" because a person or a group honed in on one point of view (Elden Ring).
- **Teaching behavior builds trust.** Some products succeed by knowing what people would like *if they tried it*, then teaching them. That behavior change creates "incredible trust": people see you as someone who knows what to make.
- **Track record can't be shortcut.** "People who are simply prompting AI will not be able to get that track record." They may get lucky when their scope happens to match what users like, but the question is whether they can deliver quality "repeatedly."

### Team composition
- "The differentiator is not just how each specific component is produced by a person. It is also what the qualitative aspect of team collaboration in a specific place with a specific group of people yields."
- "These are not linear results. You put the right creative combinations of people together, and you get wildly different results and wildly different ideas."
- "You can't under-index on people's perspective... approach... sensibilities."
- Whether the market cares about that difference is up to the market. But "if you're optimizing for differentiation, how you compose a team and how that team works and the knowledge of that team makes a big difference."
- **A failure mode:** a team made entirely of high-level world builders may ship craft that's merely adequate, because "you don't actually know what you don't know. And even if you do know, you don't actually know how to execute on it."

## Examples and anecdotes
- **Concept, lore, vibe or story as the differentiator** (low-level craft only adequate). Examples suggested in chat: *Undertale*, *Lethal Company*.
- **Low-level craft as the differentiator.** Contrast examples suggested in chat: *Cuphead* (hand-drawn animation), *Celeste* (game feel).
- **Overwatch** as a low-level differentiator: gun feel, animation and movement juice. "Overwatch isn't about lore."
- **App churn from feel:** buttons that don't quite respond, slight lag, dropping frame rate, waits that are too long. People churn "because they lose trust in the quality of the product."
- **iPhone:** "won on the qualitative stuff," like the elastic bounce at the end of a scroll (rubber-band scrolling) and responsive interaction.
- **Elden Ring:** players can tell it "looks sick" without being able to say why one design is better than another. ("chain design" in the raw dump may be a dictation slip; check what was meant.)
- Still needed: Natan's own examples, ideally from games Natan knows well or has worked on.
- **South Park:** "not funny despite it being lo-fi, cutout, fast animation. It is funny because of that." Also raised in chat: the look supports a roughly six-day production turnaround, which keeps the show topical. The limitation creates value beyond the aesthetic.

## Tensions and contradictions
- **Masterpieces vs. adequacy layers.** Parts 1 and 2 say products should find their one differentiator and let the other layers be adequate (and automated). Part 3 says the most-loved games are excellent at every layer. Both can be true, but they point at different tiers of the market:
  - Top tier (masterpieces): every layer matters, so less of it can be hollowed out.
  - Middle tier (commercially viable): one or two differentiators plus adequate everything else. This is where AI hollowing-out hits hardest. (Raised in chat.)
  - The article needs to say which tier it's about, or make the tier split the point.
- **Unknowable vs. identifiable differentiators.** Dump 2 says creators must "get smart about identifying" their differentiator. Part 4 says we often won't know why something won. If both hold, the advice becomes "protect the conditions that produce differentiation" (people, craft, team) rather than "pick your differentiator."
- **"Find your layer" vs. emergent differentiation (part 6).** If some products are differentiated only as a whole, there's no single layer to find. The advice "find the layer" then needs a second branch: sometimes the differentiator is the combination.
- **Art vs. commerce scope.** Dump 1 put art for its own sake out of scope, but part 3 uses "art" and "masterpiece" as the top of the commercial ladder. Probably fine (masterpieces also sell), but the framing should be consistent.
- **vs. "masterpieces fire on all cylinders" (dump part 3).** If the best work is excellent at every layer, where does restraint fit? Possible resolution: firing on all cylinders means every layer is *right*, not *maximal*. South Park's animation is excellent at being exactly what the show needs.
- **vs. "we won't know why it won" (dump part 4).** If success is often misattributed, a track record is a noisy signal of discernment. Luck and skill are hard to separate over a handful of releases.

## Open questions
- Does the Pareto incentive protect craft only at franchises, or also for new games aiming for the top?
- How does a creator find their differentiator *before* shipping? Indie failures suggest people often guess wrong. (Partial answer from part 3: look at what the player does most often.)
- Is "adequate" a fixed bar? If AI makes polish cheap for everyone, the floor for "adequate" may rise.
- Does this generalize beyond games? Scope part 1 said "product making" broadly, while part 2 is all games.
- "Hollow out" vs "go away": are these different outcomes?
- Which products taught users a behavior they then loved? (Candidates to check: the iPhone touch keyboard, swipe interfaces, the Souls-like difficulty curve.)
- Is the trust in the creator or the brand? Do audiences actually follow discerning creators across products?
- What does "simply prompting AI" mean exactly? No taste, or just the tool?
- Is this a separate article, or a section of the AI argument?
- What's the AI angle? If AI replaces some team members, does the team lose the perspective those people brought, not just their labor?
- Is "the right creative combination" something anyone can do on purpose, or only spotted in hindsight?

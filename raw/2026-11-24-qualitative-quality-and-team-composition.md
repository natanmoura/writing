The other thing worth noting as well is that what we're going to find is that things are going to win, and we're not going to know exactly why they won. We will attribute the wrong reasons for why they won. and that is because the differentiator is not just how each specific component is produced by a person. It is also what the qualitative aspect of team collaboration in a specific place with a specific group of people yields. These are not linear results. You put the right creative combinations of people together, and you get wildly different results and wildly different ideas. That is why you can't under-index on people's perspective. And people's approach, and people's sensibilities, because when you combine a group of people's sensibilities, approaches, and opinions together, you actually end up somewhere far more differentiated. Now, whether people care about that is up to the market to decide, but if you're optimizing for differentiation, how you compose a team and how that team works and the knowledge of that team makes a big difference. If you would compose a team where everyone is a high level world builder, you might not make a game where the craft is very good, or that it's just simply adequate, simply because you don't actually know what you don't know. And even if you do know, you don't actually know how to execute on it. So much around products is qualitative. And we like talking about the things we can measure, but it's actually the qualitative stuff that players often stay for. Even though they can't fully properly articulate it. They know Elden Ring looks sick, they don't know why, one chain design is better than another. All they know is that the sum of everything has produced something that is very differentiated, very unique. we are creatures who are highly sensitive to patterns, whether we can articulate it or not. We do notice when patterns are different. People who are artists, people who are craftspeople, they can see those differences more clearly. But if you think that the average player can't feel or sense those differences, you are severely wrong. And most people know that this is the case. Because they've interacted with products before. when we talk about apps, for example, and we talk about what actually causes a user to churn out of an app. The things we like to talk about are usually high-level things. The onboarding was bad. It wasn't a good value proposition. The price was too high. All things that are somewhat testable and somewhat capable of measuring. But the reality is there's an entire bunch of qualitative things at play that are also influencing churn. And everyone knows this because everyone's experienced it. If you open an app and the buttons aren't really working, there's slight lag, the frame rate's dropping, it's making you wait too long, it doesn't feel smooth, people churn just on that because they lose trust in the quality of the product and they don't want to pay for something low quality. If you ask someone why an iPhone is good, the reality is iPhone won on the qualitative stuff. They cared about how an elastic window bounce, I forget the name of it, felt. They cared about how interactivity felt responsive. Individually, these things don't matter, but when you add them all up together, it smells like quality. It smells like I gave a fuck. And people have a very good nose for that.

## 1. Beyond utility: story, tastemakers and longevity

Another interesting thing to think about is the high ticket item that differentiates on a vector that basically no one else is playing. It's effectively a different product. There's a reason why most sunglasses fall a certain price bracket. Most people are not going to buy something that is like over $200. That being said, there is a company that shows up and decides they're going to sell their sunglasses for $5,000, and suddenly there's a market for it. But they're not winning on sunglasses. They're winning on a story, a narrative, on craft, and exclusivity. The high-ticket item defies category, because often the high-ticket item is more about signaling than it is about utility. A lot of people buy really fancy watches, but they don't really wear them. Most of them sit in a cabinet. When something is beyond the realm of utility and is expensive enough and exclusive enough, now we are in the realm of collections. And the realm of collections often defies logic. The realm of collecting is about perception, and the value at the end of the day comes down to who is willing to pay a certain amount for something. The people who are called tastemakers are the first one to buy a thing, and then everyone else decides that they were right, and that thing is in fact worth a lot of money. Often the person selling it is not the one who is the tastemaker. They understand what makes it good, and they are able to describe what makes it unique and good, tell that story. The tastemaker is simply the first person who cares about that story enough to evangelize it. Why? Because we trust people more who evangelize something that they didn't make. If someone makes it and tells you something is great, you will say, well, obviously, because you made it, you think it's great. But the second you have a third party, or a second party that is saying, hey, this is great, and I'm willing to drop this kind of money on it, that is essentially the validation and signal that can drive the price. Now, let's not kid ourselves. A lot of this is perception driven, and perception can also create a lot of weird games that people play. In other words, creating hype, creating exclusivity that is frankly short-lived. The real high-ticket items that really command a real price are the ones that have stood the test of time. The ones where people come back to it and they say, hey, 100 years ago, this thing was valuable. I have more trust now that there is actually something intrinsically, qualitatively differentiated about this. It's not just a one-off fluke. So on a high-ticket item is the combination of this perception, but also the trust and the longevity of something also matters a lot. In some sense, you can say that in a market sense, art operates a lot like this. It is very, very specific value, and not everyone actually values that thing. But amongst the people that do, there is at least several other buyers who will value it at that price. And again, these things are leveraging on things that are frankly qualitative. What is the value of a certain person's story or perspective in history and the work that they produced at that time? These things become kind of snapshots of a time, of an era, of a way of seeing the world. And if those qualitative things are something you believe in and you like, owning it and buying things signals, again, to others how much you care about those things. A lot of people who have a lot of money appreciate and care about things. But part of it is because they are completely unable to make those things themselves. Hence we get the phenomenon of the rich living vicariously through the more authentic lives of people who are ordinary, who actually went through struggles, who actually overcame and metabolized a perspective that is far more unique than anything that they could have produced themselves. It's a type of a fetishization, you could say. And sometimes it is pure signal. It is just about signaling to the group that you, in fact, also have taste, just like others who are engaging in the same behavior. The categories where you don't justify based on utility are exactly the categories and vectors on which there's effectively no limit to how high you can sell. The only limit is how good of a story you can set, you can make. And whether you have a differentiated way of doing things that people care about.

## 1. Framing: AI replacing humans in product-making

So one of the things I want to talk about is this idea of how AI is going to replace humans, right? And I'm talking specifically about in the realm of the product making space, where really you're trying to make something and sell it at a profit. And that's where the incentive to actually cut corners is, right? We're not just making art because it's fun. We're making it because we're trying to get results. We're trying to get returns. We're trying to run a business, that kind of thing. In this realm, I think there's a lot of discussion about how, certain aspects of AI are essentially getting rid of entire parts of the pipeline. And what I want to kind of provide here is a more nuanced approach to this by really thinking about what matters and where AI and how AI is going to cause that to certain areas to hollow out and certain things to go away, but how it's not going to be an even distribution. It's actually going to be jagged, and it's actually going to be product specific.

---

## 2. Differentiation vectors

So what I want to kind of get into is the idea that at the end of the day, when a product is made, the reason it does well is because it is differentiating in some way on some vector that people actually care about. The problem with a lot of indie games, of course, is that some of them aren't that differentiated, or some of them are differentiated, but not in ways that it turns out people care about. Or enough people care about. To actually be profitable. So the question then becomes, what is it that people care about? And I think people are being too prescriptive across the board, saying things like, only the high level direction concepting and stuff like that is going to matter, and all the low level craft like 3D modeling, animation, and game feel, and all these things are going to somehow be automated away. And what I would argue is that that is not the case. What will really happen is that products and creators are going to have to get smart about identifying what it is about what they're making that is differentiated. And the truth is, everything in the game design stack can be a differentiator. For example, in some games, we actually see the lore the concept, the vibes, and the top-level story is really the differentiating factor. The low-level stuff, mostly needs to be adequate. Can't be broken, but it doesn't need to be revolutionary. In those cases, sure, you can probably do a lot of 3D automation that can probably save you a bunch of time. Maybe think of an example of a game like that.

---

## 3. Low-level craft and firing on all cylinders

On the other hand, certain games live and die on the low level. Aspect. in fact, many games the thing the player spends the most time doing is a low-level repetitive interaction. If you don't get that interaction feeling great, whether it's sword attack animations, game feel, all the juice that's involved, the kickback on a gun, all the gun animations in Overwatch, the way characters speed up when they move, effects animation, all of that juice. If you don't get that stuff right, players are not going to like your game as much. And the games that do get those things right end up winning. But the truth is, Overwatch isn't about lore. So the core idea here is that you need to differentiate in the place where it matters. And sometimes that differentiation is high level, and sometimes that differentiation is low level. The games that people love the most are often hitting on multiple cylinders, not just one. They often have everything. The things that people consider art and consider a masterpiece are often things that are firing on all cylinders. They have everything from the top level all the way down to the low level at a very high quality.

## 4. Qualitative quality and team composition

The other thing worth noting as well is that what we're going to find is that things are going to win, and we're not going to know exactly why they won. We will attribute the wrong reasons for why they won. and that is because the differentiator is not just how each specific component is produced by a person. It is also what the qualitative aspect of team collaboration in a specific place with a specific group of people yields. These are not linear results. You put the right creative combinations of people together, and you get wildly different results and wildly different ideas. That is why you can't under-index on people's perspective. And people's approach, and people's sensibilities, because when you combine a group of people's sensibilities, approaches, and opinions together, you actually end up somewhere far more differentiated. Now, whether people care about that is up to the market to decide, but if you're optimizing for differentiation, how you compose a team and how that team works and the knowledge of that team makes a big difference. If you would compose a team where everyone is a high level world builder, you might not make a game where the craft is very good, or that it's just simply adequate, simply because you don't actually know what you don't know. And even if you do know, you don't actually know how to execute on it. So much around products is qualitative. And we like talking about the things we can measure, but it's actually the qualitative stuff that players often stay for. Even though they can't fully properly articulate it. They know Elden Ring looks sick, they don't know why, one chain design is better than another. All they know is that the sum of everything has produced something that is very differentiated, very unique. we are creatures who are highly sensitive to patterns, whether we can articulate it or not. We do notice when patterns are different. People who are artists, people who are craftspeople, they can see those differences more clearly. But if you think that the average player can't feel or sense those differences, you are severely wrong. And most people know that this is the case. Because they've interacted with products before. when we talk about apps, for example, and we talk about what actually causes a user to churn out of an app. The things we like to talk about are usually high-level things. The onboarding was bad. It wasn't a good value proposition. The price was too high. All things that are somewhat testable and somewhat capable of measuring. But the reality is there's an entire bunch of qualitative things at play that are also influencing churn. And everyone knows this because everyone's experienced it. If you open an app and the buttons aren't really working, there's slight lag, the frame rate's dropping, it's making you wait too long, it doesn't feel smooth, people churn just on that because they lose trust in the quality of the product and they don't want to pay for something low quality. If you ask someone why an iPhone is good, the reality is iPhone won on the qualitative stuff. They cared about how an elastic window bounce, I forget the name of it, felt. They cared about how interactivity felt responsive. Individually, these things don't matter, but when you add them all up together, it smells like quality. It smells like I gave a fuck. And people have a very good nose for that.

## 5. Restraint, taste and track record

Going to the other side of the spectrum, we can talk about how it's not just about going more quality on everything. Sometimes the differentiation is actually the limitation. And this is where we start understanding what mastery taste and these things are actually about. It's just about, it's just as much about what you choose to do as what you choose not to do. South Park is not funny, despite it being lo-fi, cutout, fast animation. It is funny because of that. They understood that going lo-fi actually made the humor more funny. often quality and differentiation is a byproduct of restraint . That restraint is something that a lot of people identify as taste. The ability to explore a limitation, to its logical conclusion, is a powerful differentiator. just because you can add more production value doesn't mean you end up with a better product. We often like things that understand and have the wisdom to strip back to the essentials. We can feel when there is discernment in a product. Many products succeeded because they understood something that people would want if they just tried it, and then they taught people how to do it, and then people learned that they, in fact, did like it. When you create that behavioral change, that creates incredible trust. Because now people view you as someone who actually understands what to make and what people will like once it's made. We cannot shortcut this. You need a track record. People who are simply prompting AI will not be able to get that track record . They may get lucky a couple times where the limitations or the scope of what they were building happens to map very nicely to something that users like, but the question is whether they have enough discernment to really think about things and deliver that high quality repeatedly.

## 6. Thesis restatement

So the key point here is that what matters to different products or ideas and what people will care about is not some kind of evenly distributed thing. It is actually jagged. Some products will not benefit from a better 3D model. Some products will live and die on whether they can deliver on those. In the same way that some products will live and die by the game feel of the kickback of a gun, and others will not.

---

## 7. Automation in the differentiating layer

You need to find the layer in what you're building that is the differentiator . You don't need to protect it from automation, but if you are going to use automation for it, you need to make sure that it is at the actual quality that is extremely high. If you can use automation tools to arrive at really, really good game feel, then that is great. Of course, it is also true that past a certain point, there may be diminishing returns. It's also true that some games don't win on a single thing. Some. Some games are product win on an entire ecosystem of things that aren't necessarily all that differentiated or polished, but the sum of all their parts actually creates something that feels that is differentiated as a whole. As opposed to by individual parts. you need to have the discernment to understand what is actually differentiating what you do from what anyone else is doing. If you want to cut corners, no one is stopping you. But again, it comes down to your discernment . A lot of people will cut corners . Their product won't work and they will misattribute that to some other reason other than the fact that the quality simply was not there.. The reason being they will not understand what they are missing qualitatively because because It is a blind spot. A lot of people do not know what they don't know. They'll often get an AI to search up what they don't know, and they'll implement that. But if you are doing that in an area where you're trying to differentiate, you are not actually going to end up differentiating a whole lot. It only maybe works in areas where you're only looking for good enough. But I have found that even people who think something is good enough often produce stuff that is far from good enough, and not in a way that only experts understand, but in a way that the market also immediately clocks.

## 8. Misattribution, perspective and LLMs

Well, the key thing is because the qualitative stuff is so hard to measure, we see where players put their money. But because we can't measure these things, we misattribute why those things are successful. When was the last time you heard someone say, this game is good because the gun shooting feels great? Only people who really understand that stuff can tell you that more specifically. Most people will just tell you, it feels good. It feels smooth. They'll give you some kind of nebulous answer like that. But I guess that's true with any of the aspects of a game. Like, even at the high level, like the designs being cool, what does that mean, right? Why is Elden Ring's design so cool and others are not so cool? People probably can't articulate. They just know it looks cool, and they know that it's differentiated, and they know because it feels like they're in a place that is specific. That specificity comes from a perspective, or a collective group of people that are really honing in on a perspective. And this comes down to a fundamental problem with LLMs, which is they codify language. And so much of what we like about games when it comes to the look of something and the details and the feel and all these things are often not things that we can articulate. We are getting to the point where sometimes an AI can view what's been done in another game through a video and then derive the logic that arrives at that game feel, for example. But if you just pull out a design and you ask an LLM to identify why a design is good, ultimately, it doesn't have a perspective to really do that by. Perspectives, like taste, is not about just logically solving for something. It's about saying, I like this and I don't like that. And whether something succeeds or not depends on whether other people agree that they like that more than something else.

## 9. Winner-take-all, convergence and the hollowed middle

*(Added later the same day, 16:56 UTC. The second half of this message is part 5 of raw/2026-11-24-fitting-into-someones-life.md.)*

Another weird thing I think about is the Pareto distribution, the winner takes all. If the top level games succeed, and they're paying millions of dollars for people to model and do things by hand, it costs them time, but if the quality means that they are able to keep a reputation and win big, they get such outsized returns that it's actually not in their best interest to just automate everything away and create and take shortcuts. You can probably use some AI tools to help in the process, but they would probably have to be very discerning about not losing the quality, because without that, their reputation dies, and actually there's way more at stake for them to not lose that. And then, of course, if other people are using AI tools, then by them dipping into the AI tools as well, they are actually becoming more like the crowd, right? And all these micro decisions, they start converging on everything else that's out there. So it's not actually in their best interest to do that. Even if it costs them millions more, they're at risk of losing way more millions by ruining a reputation or a franchise or something that's working, right? So maybe that's only true for franchises. New games may win on other fronts, but the reality is there's only a certain amount of games that make money in a year because there's only so much demand. So now we're going to get a lot of games, but the ones that are going to win are still going to be the ones that are the highest quality. And so the question is, does AI produce good enough quality that no one would notice? Some people will make that bet, but if craft matters, then even if it costs millions more to use for craft, the top-end games will still probably opt for that because they still want to differentiate. And they can't compromise on quality. It will probably hollow out the middle, though. There will be a lot of indie creators or people who cut corners or some who find intelligent ways of using AI to scale. And I'm sure there will be some massive hits as a result, because some people will be able to automate some things away and then create something much larger at scale, much quicker because of AI. But again, it still comes down to quality. Like, do people care? Because a lot of people might step into a game and play it for a bit, but the question is, do they come back day 2, 3, 4, 5, 6? You know, if you don't have any very good retention, then yeah, you can have a flash in the pan game that is just essentially like a quick one and done. People get it, they play it for an evening.
